# M0 Teacher's Guide ｜ CFG-5 Semester Course · Lesson 0: The Kickoff — Before the First Light (v1)

_Chaihuo Maker Academy · Smart Hardware Fundamentals (M0) ｜ 45-minute kickoff session ｜ Source: 中文版 v1（`交付物/教师课件/M0_教师课件_CFG-5_第0课_先导课_点亮之前_v1.md`） ｜ Pairs with slide deck: Lesson 0, 12 slides (English deck to be produced in a later phase; page numbers below refer to the current deck)_

> **The one-sentence brief**: This lesson is all talk, no hardware. Your job is to send every student into Lesson 1 carrying their **own question**. Do it well and the next ten sessions run smoothly; do it badly and Lesson 1 starts with a morale problem you'll have to fix on your feet.

---

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

_By the end of this session, students will be able to…_

1. **Describe** what this course is — and what it is not — in one sentence. *(Bloom: Describe)*
2. **Explain** what "AI-assisted coding" means and **name** the three things they will do instead of writing code. *(Bloom: Explain, Name)*
3. **Identify** one small problem in their daily life that could become their project. *(Bloom: Identify)*
4. **State** what they will take away from the ten sessions. *(Bloom: State)*

---

## 📋 Page One: The Last Look Before You Walk In

- **Two placeholders must be filled before class** — slide 2 (your photo + one-line bio) and slide 3 (a screenshot of something **you** built with AI). This is the only "hard prep" in the whole lesson. Walking in with empty placeholders is walking in without credibility.
- **One rule to communicate all lesson**: no hand-written code, ever. Your job has exactly three parts — **say it clearly, watch what happens, tell it to change** (slide 6).
- **What NOT to cover**: no software installation, no handouts, and don't spend more than 30 seconds on "what computer do I need" (standard reply: *"You'll get a prep list before Lesson 1. Today, just listen."*).
- **Timing language**: say "ten sessions / this course" — never "ten weeks" or "three months". No fixed clock times.
- **What every student should leave with**: one sentence in their head — *"The small thing in my life that annoys me is ___."* That is the seed for Lesson 2's project pitch, and the only "homework" of this lesson (verbal, not written).

## 3. Segment by Segment

### 3.1 Welcome + the course in one sentence (3 min) 【Slide 1】 [I Do]

**Say this:**

> "Welcome to Chaihuo Maker Academy. In this course you don't need to know how to code — not a single line. Today is all talk, no hands on hardware. I'll tell you where we're going, what you'll take away, and how it works. Next time, we open the computers."

Point at the Lesson-1 outcome image on the cover:

> "That's what a student from the last cohort built in their very first lesson — their name glowing on the board's screen. By the end of your first lesson, each of you will have one too."

**CFU (quick, optional):** *"Thumbs up if you know what a 'board' is — don't worry, it doesn't matter, we'll show you. The point is: that name on the screen? That's you, next session."*

### 3.2 Who I am + a real example + an engineer does it too (9 min) 【Slides 2–4】 [I Do]

- **Slide 2 — keep it to 1 minute**: name, where you're from, why you're teaching this course. One sentence each.
- **Slide 3 is the center of gravity of this lesson**: **tell it as a story, don't read specs.** Three beats:
  1. What I was trying to solve (one specific annoyance);
  2. I wrote zero lines of code — but every line was generated from what *I* asked for. Here's how I talked to the AI;
  3. How it felt the day it worked.
- Land it with one more line:

> "The build itself is worth nothing. What's worth something is that **I now believe I can take on any 'can you make a…' question**."

- **Slide 4 (2 min — the case is already on the slide, just tell it)** — "And I'm not the only one":

> "This is Brandy Li — an application engineer at Seeed Studio, the company that makes our boards. Her job is helping other people use the boards. She uses AI every day, and the thing that annoyed her most was: interruptions — switching windows, cutting, pasting, back and forth. So she built a **voice keyboard**: hold to talk, release to send — zero lines of code.
> Watch *how* she did it — **the first version had exactly one key.** She pressed it 20 times in a row and only moved on when it never missed. Then she added keys, then recording, then the case. **A professional engineer's first version was small enough to be one key.** That's how we work in this course — and when you build your MVP in Lesson 8, you'll meet her again."

- Close slide 4 with:

> "This isn't a kids' way of doing things. This is how engineers work. Ten sessions from now, you'll work this way too."

### 3.3 Why now: what's left for you (5 min) 【Slide 5】 [I Do]

- Open with: *"AI can already write, draw, translate, and look things up for you — and it's getting faster. So what's left for you?"*
- Walk through the four things, one at a time, each with a moment from your build story (no more than 30 seconds each): **spot the problem · judge the result · understand real people · make the call**.
- Land it:

> "This course trains all four. Programming? That's the AI's job."

**CFU:** *"Turn to your neighbor: name one of the four things, the one you already feel good at. Ten seconds each way."*

### 3.4 You're the director, AI is the programmer (5 min) 【Slide 6】 [I Do] → [We Do]

- Side-by-side contrast: the **director** sets the brief, sets the standard, and accepts the work; the **programmer** writes the code, fixes the bugs, and never complains.
- Announce the house rule — light tone, frame it as good news:

> "House rule for this course: **you do not write code by hand.** If I catch you — and I will — you get to demo what you wrote for the class, and we all have a laugh. Your job is three things: **say it clearly, watch what happens, tell it to change.**"

- **We Do — read it together:** point at the screen, have the class read the three things with you, once:

> "Say it clearly — watch what happens — tell it to change."

- ⚠️ **Parent note (open-house / adult-audience sessions)** — add this line here:

> "If you're wondering 'but aren't they supposed to learn to code?' — the code can be learned any time. What we're building first is the ability to *direct* code. That's the part you can't catch up on later."

### 3.5 Your three tools (5 min) 【Slide 7】 [I Do]

- Hold up a kit if you have one, otherwise point at the screen:
  - **Grove Beginner Kit** (Lessons 1–2, and from Lesson 6 on): *"Plug in USB, and it works — ten sensors on one board. Your first board."*
  - **Wio Terminal** (Lessons 3–4, 6): *"This one gives your project a face."*
  - **XIAO ESP32S3 Sense** (Lesson 5): *"Thumbnail-sized. This one teaches your hardware to see."*
- Land it:

> "From Lesson 7 on, all three work together for your project."

**CFU:** *"Three tools, three one-liners: which one 'gives your project a face'? — Wio. Which one 'teaches it to see'? — XIAO. Nice."*

### 3.6 The ten-session map (5 min) 【Slide 8】 [I Do]

- Students don't need to memorize it — just walk through it pointing. **You** must be able to do it off script.
- ⚠️ The label for lessons 3–6 is "**team up with AI**, learn skills" and Lesson 4 is "team up with AI, build a pomodoro timer" — the "team" here means giving **AI** roles, **not** putting students into groups. Say the word "AI" out loud when you read it.
- **Emphasize only the two milestones:**

> "Lesson 7 — you claim your own project. From that day, the problem you're solving is *yours*. Lesson 10 — you stand up and present your project to the whole room."

- Student asks for details (which software in which lesson, do I need to buy a board)? → *"We'll cover that at the start of each lesson. Nothing to remember today."*

### 3.7 The 8-step project loop + two things you'll walk away with (4 min) 【Slides 9–10】 [I Do]

- **Slide 9** — read the eight steps once, pointing at each: *Frame the problem → Find the user → Prototype → Code → Make trade-offs → Test & judge → Document → Share the value.* Then the one line that matters:

> "Most courses only teach step 4 — writing code. This course walks all eight steps, and at every step, AI is your partner."

- **Slide 10** — the two takeaways:
  - **Six skill cards**: spot a problem · find the user · build it · make trade-offs · judge quality · tell the story. *"Every time you do one, you light up a square in your log. By the end, all six are lit."*
  - **A full set of AI-prompting phrases**: *"These are the exact ways of talking to AI so it understands you. When we use one in class, we read it off the screen together, and every phrase lives in your Student Workbook. At the end of the course you take the whole set with you — for homework, for school projects, for anything."*
- The closing line:

> "Phrases are scaffolding, not a boss. What you build — and whether it's good — that's always your call."

### 3.8 What you take away (3 min) 【Slide 11】 [I Do]

Four things, quickly: **a project of your own · its story (ten sessions of logs, gathered into a README) · your 2-minute showcase · a method you can reuse.**

Land it:

> "For competitions, for interviews, for showing your family and friends — the growth story is worth more than the build itself."

### 3.9 Start thinking now (4 min) 【Slide 12】 — the memory point of the lesson [I Do]

- Slow your pace:

> "Your project starts with a question: **What's one small thing in your daily life that annoys you?**"

- Read three examples — *the classroom light nobody turns off at night / a family member who keeps forgetting their pills / plants that never get watered* — then **stay silent for 30 seconds** and let them actually think.

> **[Wait time: 30 s — do not fill the silence. Let it sit.]**

- Land it:

> "You don't need to answer now. Just remember it — better yet, jot it down in your phone notes tonight. In Lesson 2, we turn it into your project."

### 3.10 Next lesson preview + wrap (2 min) 【Slide 13】 [I Do]

> "Next lesson: light up your first piece of hardware with AI. You say one sentence, AI writes the instruction, and the board comes alive in three minutes. By the end of the lesson, your name will be glowing on the screen. Bring your problem. See you then."

## 4. Backup Plans at a Glance

| Risk | Plan B |
| --- | --- |
| You forgot to fill in your build screenshot | Two paths: ① use a Lesson-1 outcome demo on slide 3 and fill it in later; ② skip slide 3 entirely and make slide 4 (the Brandy case) your main story — full script in 3.2, image already on the slide. **Students must never see an empty placeholder page** |
| Projector / laptop fails | Slides 5/6/8/12 are the skeleton — they work with no screen at all (the four things / director & programmer / two milestones / the question). Skip everything else |
| A student asks "what kind of projects can we make?" | Give 2 examples at the "Lesson 7 wall" level (e.g., *a box that reminds grandpa to take his pills*), don't expand, and steer to slide 12 |
| Parents are in the room | Use the parent note in 3.4 and the one in 4.4 |

### 4.3 Network / Equipment Downgrade

This lesson depends on nothing but the projector. No network, no software, no boards required. If the projector dies, teach the four skeleton slides above and wrap on time — don't hold the class hostage waiting for equipment.

### 4.4 Parent Note (flow)

For open-house and info-session audiences, add one note after 3.4 and one after 3.8:

- **After 3.4:** *"You might be thinking: what ARE they learning if not code? This course trains four things — spotting problems, judging results, understanding people, making decisions. AI writes the code; those four are exactly what AI can't hand them. Code can be picked up any time; this order, if reversed, is hard to fix."*
- **After 3.8:** *"What you'll see after ten sessions is not a homework assignment. It's a working project they can demo live, a README that records how it grew, and two minutes on stage where they explain it clearly."*

## 5. Pitfall Speed Sheet

| In-class situation | How to handle it |
| --- | --- |
| "Does this cost money / do I have to buy a board?" | *"The kit is provided in class — nothing to buy."* One line, no prices |
| "My computer is old — is that okay? / What computer do I need?" | *"You'll get a prep list before Lesson 1. Today, just listen."* — no more than 30 seconds |
| "I already know Python." | Catch it: *"Excellent — you'll move faster. Same rule though: no hand-written code. Your job here is directing. You can take on harder features."* |
| Someone blurts out an answer during the slide-12 silence | Smile, listen to one sentence, say *"Remember it — we'll use it in Lesson 2"*, and keep the silence going. Don't open any idea for discussion on the spot |
| You're running long | Cut the detail on slides 9–10. Protect slide 12 |
| Info session: asked about teachers / certificates / competitions | *"Let's talk after the session."* — never expand on the spot |
