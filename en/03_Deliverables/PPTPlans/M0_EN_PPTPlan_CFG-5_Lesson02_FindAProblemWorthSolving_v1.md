## Design Principles

- **The one mental model carries over from Lesson 1**: **Sense → Logic → Output**. Today it's the through-line — slide 02 recalls it, slide 17's design sheet asks students to fill their own three boxes before they build, and slide 18's build time lives under it. No new mental model is introduced today; the deck re-uses the one students already own.
- **One cover-grade page**: **slide 03 — the Three Kinds of Projects map** (8-minute hard timebox). Icons, color blocks and shape composition carry the page; text is cut to the minimum. It is the students' inspiration page for the whole session — they will point at it all day. The kind-② (computer's partner) examples must be ones the instructor rehearsed and ran (Rehearsal 5) — unrehearsed examples do not go on the page beyond a concept mention.
- **Output anchors everywhere**: slides 04 / 06 / 07 / 08 / 09 / 17 / 20 each carry a 📝 "write it in your workbook (or notebook)" action box — every segment's output lands in the student's own record. The red-line gate is set on slide 05: *"the blanks in a phrase must be filled with your own stuff — a phrase sent with blanks doesn't count."*
- **Two-track design (kit / no-kit)**: slides 16 and 18 run in dual tracks — with the parts box, wiring play + the 16:21 wiring-check call-out; without it, onboard modules only, and the call-out becomes TA circulation. The lesson works exactly the same either way.
- **Stand-still pages**: during hands-on time the projected page stays fixed — the screen is the students' working reference, not the instructor's teleprompter. Stand-still pages: 06 (students send their phrase), 13 (rule experiments), 18 (the 51-minute build).
- **Zero print**: phrase sets are NOT handed out — they live in the Student Workbook; project and read together when they debut (slide 05), point students to the book when they forget.
- **Demo-rehearsal discipline**: five live demos today (idea generation / Five-Rules comparisons / parts-box wiring / Picky User / kind-② example). Anything that puts text on screen is rehearsed in English/numbers/graphics — no gambling on font rendering. Live demo fails → cut to backup within 30 seconds. **Not an incident.**

### Slide 07 | Ten Is Too Many — Keep One: The Tough Reviewer — Demo guide page

**Type**: Demo guide · **Time**: 8 min

**On screen** (verbatim):

```text
Ten Is Too Many — Keep One
The Tough Reviewer

(monospace phrase card, excerpt)
You are the bluntest advisor who still wants me to succeed. Here are my ideas: ___.
Grill me with three questions, one by one: Can it be done in 3 hours?
Do I have the hardware? Would I really use it every day?

① Can it be done in 3 hours?
② Do I have the hardware?
③ Would I really use it every day?

```

**Instructor notes** (verbatim): *"Ten is too many — you can only build one. Meet today's bluntest phrase: the Tough Reviewer — AI plays the rudest, most honest advisor who still wants you to win. Getting grilled isn't shameful — it kills the ideas that can't stand up, before you've spent half a day on them. Go."* **Today's number-one pitfall lives here**: AI says pick X, the student picks X. ### Slide 13 | 3 Minutes Each: 30 s Talk + 2 min Experiment + 30 s Show the Result — Operation page

**Type**: Operation · **Time**: 15 min (3 min per rule; stays fixed during experiments)

**On screen** (verbatim):

```text
Each Rule, 3 Minutes: 30 s Talk + 2 min Experiment + 30 s Show the Result

① One thing at a time
   first: "turn on the LED, beep the buzzer, and show something on the screen, all at once"
   then: split it into three sentences, one at a time — see the difference yourself

② Say the input & the output
   first: "make a reminder"
   then: "if no movement is detected for 1 minute, make the LED blink" — which one is precise?

③ Give it an example
   "show numbers on the screen like an elevator does" — one comparison beats ten descriptions

④ If it's wrong, add a line
   break the effect you just made on purpose, then add one line to fix it — time it

⑤ Ask it to explain
   throw the code it just wrote back at it: "what is this doing? in plain words"

(fast/slow lane, yellow) Fast lane: add the Rule 3 advanced — one comparison, flashier display.
Slow lane: Rules 1, 2 and 4 are enough.
```

**Instructor notes** — the opening line for each rule is in the Teacher's Guide (14:56–15:11); run the experiment steps projected as you go. **Rule 2's experiment is the easiest teaching moment** — a vague request produces an absurd result; circulate, collect the most absurd one, project it, let the class laugh, then land the point: *"It's guessing. Your project is ten times more complex than this — you can't afford the wrong guess."* Short on time? Cut the Rule 3 and 5 experiments — and the rules get cut with them: point at the whiteboard and say *"try these two at home"*, don't force it. Both sides of a comparison come out equally well (AI too clever)? *"Today it guessed your mind right — but your real project is ten times more complex. You can't afford the wrong guess."* Then add a two-condition request (*"only light up when it's dark AND there's sound"*) and run it again.

**Assets**: IMG Rule 1 comparison screenshots · IMG Rule 2 absurd "make a reminder" result (rehearsal backups; students' own absurd results project best).
