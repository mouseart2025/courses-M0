## 1. What You Walk Out With Today

Last session you proved "I can make things." From today, the question changes: **make things for who?**

By the end of the session, you'll have four things:

1. **A one-line project manifesto of your own** — "I'm making a ___ for ___, because ___" — on the classroom Project Wall, and copied into this workbook;
2. **A three-box design sheet** — your first design document: project name, who it's for, Sense → Logic → Output;
3. **A working build aimed at your own topic** — at least two modules working together, plus one recorded "change it or not" decision after feedback;
4. **An AI learning log** — the same four sentences, but today's "then" must say how you handled that one flaw.

Six skills, three practiced today: **spot a problem, build it, make trade-offs.**

How today works: **the teacher guides + this workbook + conversations with AI → your own conclusions and results.** Everything the teacher asks you to write goes into this workbook (online doc or paper — typing on a computer, or handwriting, both fine). It gets handed in after class, and it gets used at the start of next session.

## 2. What Your Board Can Make: The Three Kinds of Projects

Before hunting for a topic, see what kinds of things this board can actually make. Three kinds:

1. **A standalone device** — the board is the whole thing: it senses, decides, reacts, all by itself, nothing else attached. The night light, an alarm, a desk weather station, a pomodoro timer, a whack-a-mole game — all kind one.
2. **The computer's partner** — the board and the computer make something together: the board is the hands and the ears, the computer is the screen. Like turning the board into a game controller — buttons, knob, a shake — controlling a game on the screen. Or a presentation clicker: press a button, the slide on the computer turns.
3. **The outside world** — the modules can snap off and recombine; later you can add new modules, even the internet. If your class has them, the parts box is the door in (not every classroom does — if you don't, that's fine too). Today, just know this road exists.

In class we looked at one real example of each kind — your topic can land in any of them. **What matters isn't how advanced it is — it's whose annoyance it solves.**

## 4. Today's New Weapons: Two Phrase Sets

Last session you learned two spells — one thing at a time, and if it's wrong, add one more line. Today you add two new phrase sets: the **topic set** (four phrases) helps you think clearly about **what to make**; the **user set** (three phrases) helps you think clearly about **who it's for**.

**Full text right here — flip back whenever you forget.** The old rule: the blanks in a phrase must be filled with your own stuff — a phrase sent with blanks doesn't count. AI doesn't know how old your sister is.

### The "topic" set

**Phrase 1 ｜ Turn my annoyances into ideas** (once you have an annoyances list — diverge)

```
I have three real annoyances: 1. ________ 2. ________ 3. ________.
Give me 10 ideas that small hardware (sensors) could solve, the more specific the better.
Just list ideas — don't choose for me.
```

**Phrase 2 ｜ How else could this idea work?** (when an idea gets rejected, or you want to reshape it)

```
I have an idea: ________. Help me think of 3 different ways to make it,
and say how each differs from the original. Don't make it bigger —
each one must be something I can try today.
```

**Phrase 3 ｜ The Tough Reviewer** (converge from 10 ideas to 1)

```
You are the bluntest advisor who still wants me to succeed. Here are my ideas: ________.
Grill me with three questions, one by one: Can it be done in 3 hours?
Do I have the hardware? Would I really use it every day?
Then point out the one that stands up best — but the choice is mine.
```

**Phrase 4 ｜ Why I chose it** (after choosing — say the reason clearly)

```
I chose: ________. My reason: ________ (must be your own words).
Help me polish this into a one-line manifesto: "I'm making a ___ for ___, because ___."
Don't invent a reason for me.
```

Memory hook: **AI produces the quantity, you produce the decision.** Can't say "why I chose it"? You haven't chosen.

### The "user" set

**Phrase 5 ｜ Who would use it** (after choosing — list real users)

```
My project is: ________. List 2–3 specific, named people who would really use it
(like "my mom" or "my classmate Liam"), and say why each would use it.
Don't write "all students" or "everyone".
```

**Phrase 6 ｜ Help me make an interview script** (before talking to a real person)

```
I'm going to ask ________ (a real person) about this: ________. Write me 5 questions.
Rules: don't lead them into flattery ("do you like my idea" is a bad question) —
ask about their real habits and their real annoyances.
```

**Phrase 7 ｜ Pretend to be my user** (practice when no real person is available — real people first)

```
Play ________ (a specific person, traits: ________). Answer my interview the way they
would — with their habits, their complaints. At the end, tell me: which of my questions
were good, and which one was bad.
```

Memory hook: **real people > AI.** This is the substitute, not the main move.

## 6. Converge: The Tough Reviewer

Ten is too many — you can only build one. Throw the idea list at Phrase 3 and let AI grill you, idea by idea, with three questions:

1. Can it be done in 3 hours?
2. Do you have the hardware?
3. Would you really use it every day?

Getting grilled isn't shameful — it kills the ideas that can't stand up, before you've spent half a day on them. Everything rejected? No panic: take the one you can't let go of, reshape it with Phrase 2, then screen it again.

**One trap to watch for**: if AI flat-out tells you "pick this one", don't swallow it whole. Answer yourself one question first: **the one it recommends — can I say why it's good?** If you can't, run the three questions yourself one more time. The topic is your decision, not its.

**Write here: the 2 surviving ideas + one line each on "why I kept it":**

1. Idea: ______________ — why kept: ________________________________
2. Idea: ______________ — why kept: ________________________________

## 7. Who Would Use It: Real Names Only

Topic set — next question: who will use it? Use Phrase 5 and let AI help you list them — but there's a hard rule: **real names only.**

- Pass: "my mom", "my classmate Liam"
- Fail: "all students", "everyone"

A project written for "everyone" ends up used by no one. Write a name, and you know who you're finding flaws for, and who you're changing things for.

**Pair interviews**: interview each other, two at a time, 4 minutes each — you introduce your topic to your neighbor; your neighbor plays your user and asks you the questions AI wrote in Phrase 6. Embarrassed to be interviewed? Swap roles — let your neighbor interview you first.

**Write here: your one-line user picture:**

> My project is for ________, because ______________________.

Two new words (use them today): **user** — the real person who'll actually use your project; **interview** — going to ask that person a few questions.

## 9. The Five Rules for Talking to AI

First, look at two real conversations from last session: one worked on the first try, one took five rounds of back and forth. Same AI, same classroom — the difference isn't luck, it's **how you say it**. Talking to AI is like briefing a new colleague — they're smart, but they don't know what's in your head.

Five rules. Verify each one on your own board with your own hands (iron rule: **no experiments, no rules**). **The rhythm for each: 30 seconds listening + 2 minutes experimenting + 30 seconds showing your result.**

**Rule 1 ｜ One thing at a time**
Stuff three wishes into one sentence and it falls apart. Experiment: first send *"turn on the LED, beep the buzzer, and show something on the screen, all at once"* — see what you get; then split it into three sentences, one at a time. See the difference yourself.

**Rule 2 ｜ Say the input & the output**
Say two things clearly: when (the input), and what happens (the output). Experiment: first send *"make a reminder"* — see what absurd thing it hands you; then send *"if no movement is detected for 1 minute, make the LED blink"*. Which one is precise?

**Rule 3 ｜ Give it an example**
When you can't describe it, give a comparison. Experiment: send *"show numbers on the screen like an elevator does"* — one comparison beats ten descriptions. Now find a comparison for your own effect.

**Rule 4 ｜ If it's wrong, add a line — don't restart**
The old rule from last session: don't delete the conversation, don't restart — add one more line. Experiment: break the effect you just made on purpose, then add a line to fix it — time it, and see how much faster it is than starting over.

**Rule 5 ｜ Ask it to explain**
AI is a teacher too. Experiment: throw the code it just wrote back at it, and ask *"what is this doing? in plain words."* Not understanding isn't shameful — not asking is the real loss.

Fast finishers: add the Rule 3 advanced version (use one comparison to make AI show a flashier display effect). Falling behind? Keep Rules 1, 2 and 4 solid — that's a pass.

**Write here: my experiment record** (which rule made the biggest difference? what did you see with your own eyes)

- The most dramatic difference was Rule ___, I saw: ________________________________
- The one that hit me hardest today was Rule ___, because: ________________________________

## 11. The Brief: Build the First Working Piece of Your Topic

Two options — pick one and start:

1. **Cut a small slice out of your own topic and build it** (recommended) — can't find a slice? Ask the teacher or a TA to help map it "near enough" onto a starter brief; it becomes the practice piece for your topic;
2. **No direction yet? Pick one of the three starter briefs** — Reminder / Night Light / Alarm — reference combinations on the back.

**Two hardware paths, depending on what your classroom has:**

- **With the parts box**: today's modules get wired by you. The rule is one sentence — **one end into the module, the other into the socket on the edge of the board; plug it in wrong and nothing burns — just flip it around.** The only forbidden move: forcing it.
- **No parts box**: use the modules already on your board — last session's old friends are all there: light, sound, button, LED, buzzer, screen. Two linked modules, no problem.

## 13. Build Time: 40 Minutes

Start. Three rules, same as ever:

1. One thing at a time;
2. If it's wrong, add one more line;
3. Stuck? Ask your AI first, then your neighbor, then raise your hand.

Three reminders:

- **Cut a small slice and make it solid first** — directors shoot one scene at a time: get the link working, then decorate.
- **Two modules doing their own separate things doesn't count** — make the one on the left tell the one on the right something. Like: "when the light dims, it **tells** the LED: time to light up."
- Midway, the teacher will call one whole-room "stop, 10 seconds" — check against your design sheet: are you still building the thing you wrote? If you drifted, pull yourself back.

Really can't get the link working? The passing bar drops to "one build + one record of the linking attempt" — write the sticking point into your log, and it still counts as done.

## 14. AI Review: Ask It to Find One Flaw

Don't put your build away yet. Today, a new segment for the first time: **review — asking someone to find the flaws in your project.** Today's reviewer is AI, but it plays a real person — the real person named on your design sheet.

**The review phrase** (fill it in and send):

```
Please play ________ (the real name on your design sheet), and find exactly ONE flaw in
my project. Just one.
```

Two rules:

1. **Exactly one flaw** — the first review experience must be "useful and painless". Don't get greedy; one at a time, you can't eat more than that.
2. Review isn't abstract fault-finding — it's **finding flaws for that one real person**.

AI's flaw too harsh, or too absurd? Remember: **AI's opinion gets reviewed too** — do you think it's right? Getting a flaw pointed out isn't shameful — it wants your project to be worthy of the person who'll use it.

(This phrase has a formal name — the **Picky User** — Lesson 4 expands it in full. Today, just use it.)

## 15. Accept or Reject: Your First Trade-Off Decision

You've got the flaw. Now make a decision: **change it, or not.**

- **Change it** — do it right now;
- **Don't change it** — tell your neighbor the reason.

Accepting earns credit, **and so does rejecting — as long as the reason is yours.** Can't say why ("I just want to change it")? Go back and think for one minute. A reason like "my user doesn't need it" — that's judgment. A reason like "I don't feel like it" — you know it yourself.

**Write here: my trade-off decision**

> I accepted/rejected AI's suggestion that ______________________,
> because ______________________________.

This line is your **proof of being the director**.

## 16. Flash Show & Tell + Today's Log

**Show & tell**: in your table, 30 seconds each — demo your build, and say one line: "what flaw AI found, and whether I changed it."

**Log**: the same 30-second ritual, one extra step today.

**Step 1: Take one photo.** Your build together with your design sheet — two pieces of paper, one board, all made by you.

**Step 2: Talk for thirty seconds — all four sentences.** Today's "then" must include how you handled the flaw:

```
I made ________;
I got stuck on ________;
then ________ (AI found ___, I decided ___, because ___);
next time I want ________.
```

**Step 3: Let AI tidy it up — you proofread.** Send what you said to AI:

```
Tidy this into a learning log — only what I said, nothing I didn't say.
```

When it's tidied, **you must read it once before you confirm** — AI will make things up with a straight face. Proofreading is your job, and nobody can do it for you.
