## 3. Today's New Words (in plain language)

**README** — your project's "self-introduction + growth story." Someone who never touches the project, reading only this, still understands what it is and how it came to be. It's assembled from your ten logs — AI organizes, you proofread.

**The cost & value math** — two lines only: **what it cost** (materials) and **what it's worth** (your price + one sentence of "why"). Note: today counts exactly these two — no profits, no business talk. Cost is arithmetic; value is expression.

**Pitch (one page)** — the page that makes people understand in 2 minutes and want it. Four boxes: problem / solution / who it's for / what it's worth.

**The AI investor rehearsal** — have AI play an investor "who understands nothing about technology, interrupts anytime, and only asks 'So what? What's it to me?'" Getting stumped here costs nothing — **spend your fear here, and the stage holds none.**

**The release check** — the final review: no fault-picking, one question only — "can it face people?" One item on the list must have an answer: **"how do I save it if it fails?"**

## 4. Act 1 · The Final Leg (fix-only)

### Step 1: Stand-up

Read your baton handoff sheet, then one sentence:

> "I'll fix ______, and get the demo steady at ______."

⚠️ The discipline is four words: **fix-only.** A new feature wants in? The "later" list welcomes it.

### Step 2: The 45-minute sprint

Only two jobs:

1. Fix the faults the reviews left behind;
2. **Polish demo stability** — five clean demos in a row — that's what "steady" means. At the roadshow it must run through, first try, in front of strangers.

Shell still unfinished? Lesson 9's four moves — close what 10 minutes can close; what can't close, take the stage as is — **function is the required question.**

### Step 3: The release check

```text
Based on the current state: my project can ___ now, and can't ___ yet.
You're a release inspector: in one hour it will demo in front of strangers.
Give me a "final pre-release checklist": every item to confirm before the
demo (enough power? cables seated? what's the first demo step?
how do I save it if it fails?).
```

Tick every item. **"How do I save it if it fails" must have an answer** — no answer means fall back to backups: demo photos/video from your logs.

> 💡 **The engineer's same checklist:** Brandy (the Seeed engineer from Lesson 8's appendix) ran these four before sharing her voice keyboard: ① does the light come on immediately? ② does the recording end properly? ③ is the recognized text complete? ④ did the prompt land in the right window? The first two ask "is it actually working?"; the last two ask "is the work complete?" — **map them onto your project's equivalents and copy them into your checklist.** Her fallback plan: serial logs, screen recording, a finished video — your "how do I save it" is exactly that.

A real fault you can't finish fixing? Into the README's "not perfect yet" — **confessing flaws is a plus.**

## 5. Act 2 · Tell the World

### Step 4: README (ten logs become one)

Ten lessons, ten logs. Today they merge. The old way: AI organizes, you proofread:

```text
These are my ten lesson logs: (paste all logs)
Using only these logs, organize them into a README with this template:
what it's called / whose problem it solves / how to use it (3 steps max) /
how it grew (pick 3 turning points) / what's not perfect yet /
what I'd still add.
Do not add anything I didn't write.
```

After it generates, proofread sentence by sentence: **did it invent anything?** Catch it, delete it — AI will invent things with a straight face, and proofreading is your job. Ten lessons in — this is the last on-site exam.

The README template (after AI organizes, copy the skeleton out by hand):

```text
# My Project README
## What it's called:
## Whose problem it solves (a real person's name):
## How to use it (3 steps max):
## How it grew (3 turning points: the brief / the stuck / the fix):
## What's not perfect yet (honestly, 1–2 lines):
## What I'd still add (pick from the "later" list):
```

### Step 5: The cost & value math

Follow the projected method, two steps:

```text
Count exactly two things:
One. What it cost — the materials list (shell materials count, even a takeout box).
Two. What it's worth — I think it's worth ___, because ___.
(A high price is fine — but the reason must be "it solves this problem,"
not "I want it.")
```

1. List your **materials** — which components, which shell materials; have AI check for anything missed;
2. **Write one number + one reason.**

⚠️ Pricing above cost is allowed — but the reason for "expensive" must be "it solves this problem," not "I want it." Can't make it round? Lower the price until it does.

### Step 6: The one-page pitch

Four boxes, two sentences each at most. **Three hard references — can't be faked:**

```text
# My One-Page Pitch

## The problem (who is stuck with what — your requirements sheet's own words):

## The solution (how my project fixes it + the demo move):

## Who it's for (a real person from my Lesson-2 list):

## What it's worth (quoting my account: it cost ___, I think it's worth ___, because ___)
```

### Step 7: The AI investor rehearsal

```text
You are an investor who understands nothing about technology.
I'll read you my one-page pitch. You may interrupt me anytime,
but you may only ask two kinds of questions: "So what?" and
"What's it to me?" If you don't understand my answer,
keep pressing until you do.
```

Stumped → revise the pitch → read again. If it praises you, push back: "No praise. Only 'so what?' questions."

Every "couldn't answer" is a gift: **turn it into one sentence of the pitch, and nobody on stage can stump you.**

## 6. Act 3 · The Showcase & Graduation

### Step 8: The live roadshow

Three rules:

```text
The three roadshow rules:
One. Demo first, then talk — the thing really moving says more than anything.
Two. 2 minutes each — the hourglass exempts nobody.
Three. Every desk rotates; each desk sends one person to the stage.
```

⚠️ The hourglass exempts nobody — can't finish in 2 minutes = not thought through yet; finish this sentence and stop.

### Step 9: The vote

```text
The audience's job: 3 votes for the project you'd most want to see really
exist — you can't vote for yourself.
```

⚠️ Afraid of the stage? Speak at your desk first; still can't — go up with your neighbor (you demo, they talk). **The floor is: don't be absent.**

### Step 10: The closing ceremony

After the vote, the last 15 minutes of ten lessons:

- **Ten lessons, all lit** — lit the first lamp → found a question worth doing → the project grew a screen → got an AI team → taught hardware to see → moved it home → briefed it → ran the first lap → called in reinforcements → **today, told its story;**
- Remember Lesson 1? You lit your first hardware, and the screen showed your name. Today your name is on your project, in the README, in the applause — **you're not watching the magic anymore; you're making it;**
- The last log + the group photo (the old rule: hold up your project, hands up, backs to the camera — **hands only, never faces**).

## 8. When You're Stuck

| Stuck here | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| The project still has faults | Into "not perfect yet" in the README. Confessing is a plus. |
| It breaks right before going on | Play the backup video/photos + talk live — that's "demo-backup awareness." |
| Not enough logs for a README | Add two "photo + thirty seconds" entries against the project, then generate. |
| The README reads better than my actual story | Careful — AI is inventing. Ask, line by line, "did I write this in a log?" |
| No idea how to price | Pick any number — then make the "why" round; if it won't go round, lower it. |
| The investor stumped me | Great. Turn what I couldn't answer into one sentence of the pitch. |
| I'm afraid of the stage | Desk first → pair-up with your neighbor → the floor is: don't be absent. |
| Can't fit in 2 minutes | Cut "solution" details; keep "problem" and the demo. What you can't say clearly isn't the point. |
